Sprechen, rethought: a partner you actually talk to

Today nothing in the Sprechen area records a learner's voice. This review sets out what is actually there now, proposes one design thesis, prices the three technical ways of building it, and shows three named layouts for the live conversation screen. Pick one layout by name (and one pipeline), and I will build it.

2 · The thesis

Sprechen should be Schreiben with a microphone. The app already knows how to take a learner's own production, send it to an AI, and hand back a correction the learner recognises. Speaking is that same loop, one modality further out. That gives us a shape to copy rather than a new one to invent, and it is the reason a spoken correction should look exactly like a written one.

But an open ChatGPT-style voice chat is not, by itself, good practice. A large language model will happily talk to a B1 learner forever: it adapts down to their level, never corrects unless asked, has no task to complete and produces no assessment. The learner leaves fluent in the conversation they just had and nowhere else. So the feature is not "a chatbot with a voice". It is:

  • A brief. A real situation with a named partner, a role, a register (du/Sie), a level, and 2–4 things you must get done. This is the same law Schreiben already lives by: only a task carrying a full brief is served, because the AI grades task completion against it.
  • A conversation. The partner stays in character, stays at the learner's level, pushes back, and does not correct mid-flow (interrupting to correct is what kills speaking confidence).
  • A debrief. All the correction lands at the end, in the correction card the learner already knows from Schreiben: coral marks on what they said, green on the fix, Himmelblau fix tiles.

One consequence worth stating plainly: the 645 hand-authored dialogue nodes stop being a script and become briefs. Their title, task, context and target Redemittel are exactly the material the AI partner needs as its role instructions, so the authoring work is not thrown away. The branching tree underneath retires. Scenario ids stay, as they must.

3 · How the voice actually works, and what it costs

There are three ways to build "talk to an AI". They differ enormously in cost, and the difference is the single most important thing on this page, because the app currently runs under a $5-per-month global AI spending cap.

PipelineHow it worksFeels likeCostEffort
A · Browser ears and voice
recommended first build		 The browser transcribes speech for free, sends the text to our server, an AI writes the partner's reply, and the browser reads it aloud. No audio ever leaves the device. A slightly patient conversation. Roughly 1.5–3 seconds between you stopping and the partner answering. ≈ 2–4 cents for a whole 12-turn conversation including the debrief, and effectively 0 while the free Gemini Flash tier in our existing cascade absorbs the turns. Medium. One new server function; the microphone wrapper already exists.
B · Cloud ears and voice We record short audio clips, upload them, a cloud service transcribes them and reads the reply back in a natural voice. Noticeably better at understanding accented German, and a far nicer partner voice. Same 2–3 second pauses. Roughly 10–20 cents per conversation. Higher. Audio upload, storage and a retention rule, which means the privacy policy changes in the same release.
C · Real-time voice
the ChatGPT voice-mode feel		 An open audio connection straight to the model. It hears you as you speak and can be interrupted. An actual phone call. Around a third of a second of lag; you can talk over it. 5–10 cents per minute with careful caching, up to 18–46 cents per minute without. A six-minute conversation can cost more than a dollar, i.e. more than a fifth of the current monthly cap for one learner, once. Highest, and it needs its own commercial answer (a paid tier) before it can ship.

My recommendation: build A, structure it so B is a one-function swap, and hold C for a paid tier. A gets a real speaking feature in front of learners at a cost the current cap can absorb, and the layout you pick below is identical in all three cases. The one honest limitation of A is browser support: it works in Chrome, Edge and Safari but not Firefox, so a typed fallback stays for everyone else. That fallback is the current experience, so nobody ends up worse off than today.

The budget. Schreiben already meters AI use per learner per day (10 Fokus, 4 Kurz, 2 Lang). Sprechen joins that system with its own allowance. My proposal is 2 conversations per day, which sits alongside Lang and keeps the worst case predictable.

Why this screen exists. It is what turns a chat into an exercise. The partner has a name and a job, the register is stated, and the three Leitpunkte are the thing the AI grades at the end. Without them the debrief has nothing to measure.

Everything here already exists in the content bank. Title, task, context and target Redemittel are fields the 645 authored scenarios already carry. The partner persona and the Leitpunkte are the one thing that needs authoring, and they can be derived from the existing task text for a first pass.

The daily allowance is stated up front, not discovered by hitting it. That is the same fix Schreiben got: "Heute noch 2 von 2" sits beside the start button.

The microphone permission line is a legal requirement, not chrome. It sits as a standalone muted line below the card, which is where AI and legal notes live everywhere else in the app.

5 · The live conversation: three layouts

This is the actual decision. All three do the same thing and cost the same to run. They differ in what the learner looks at while they are talking, and that changes what the learner practises.

Option A

Gespräch — the chat thread

The familiar shape. A scrolling transcript of what was said, with the microphone in a fixed cluster at the bottom. This is what ChatGPT and Gemini look like.

SB

Frau Berger

Oje, gute Besserung. Und was machen wir mit dem Termin um zwei?
Ich könnte Herrn Weber fragen ob er

Ich höre zu … tippe zum Stoppen

The page does not scroll; the transcript is the one inner scrolling region.

What it is good at. Nobody needs to be taught how to use it. The learner can always see what the microphone heard, which matters a lot, because mis-transcription is the one thing that makes a speaking feature feel broken. "Falsch gehört?" lets them fix a word rather than argue with the machine.

What it costs you. It invites reading. A B2 learner who can see the German written down will read it instead of listening, and that is the opposite of what a speaking exercise is for. It is also the least distinguishable from every other AI chat app the learner already has on their phone.

Rules it respects. One inner region scrolls and hands the scroll on at its top; the page itself rests at zero. The mic cluster is the Schreiben floating cluster geometry: no bar behind it, every control on its own opaque backing.

Build: the largest of the three, because a growing transcript with replay, edit and an autoscroll that behaves on a mobile keyboard is genuinely fiddly.

Option B

Bühne — one turn at a time

No transcript at rest. The screen holds exactly what is being said right now: their line, large, and yours underneath as it comes out. History collapses into a ribbon you can open.

Phone · mid-conversation

SB

Frau Berger

Oje, gute Besserung. Und was machen wir mit dem Termin um zwei?

Du

Ich könnte Herrn Weber fragen, ob er

Ich höre zu … tippe zum Stoppen

Fixed stage. Never scrolls at any turn count, the same trick the exam uses.

What it is good at. It puts the learner's attention on one German sentence and one answer. There is no wall of text to skim, so listening is the only way forward, which is the point of a speaking exercise. It is also the only one of the three whose height is fixed, so it cannot grow past a phone screen however long the conversation runs.

It also reads as ours. This is the exam's stage shape and the Bibliothek's card language, not a cloned messenger. A learner would not mistake it for something they already have.

What it costs you. The learner cannot glance back at what was said three turns ago without opening the ribbon. For a learner who has lost the thread, that extra tap arrives at the worst moment.

Build: the smallest of the three. One turn on screen means no autoscroll, no keyboard-avoidance and no transcript virtualisation.
Option C

Anruf — no text at all

A phone call. While you are talking there is nothing to read: a partner, a sound level, a timer. Every word appears for the first time in the debrief afterwards.

Phone · mid-call

SB

Sabine Berger

Teamleiterin

02:14

Untertitel Aus

Frau Berger spricht …

Never scrolls. The least content of the three by a wide margin.

What it is good at. It is the only one that rehearses the real thing. Nobody gets subtitles at the Ausländerbehörde or on the phone to a Hausarzt, and the anxiety a learner feels in those rooms comes precisely from having nothing to read. This trains that. It also makes the debrief land hard, because it is the first time the learner sees what they actually said.

What it costs you. It is the most likely to be abandoned mid-way by a learner who cannot follow. The "Untertitel" switch is the escape hatch, resting off, and I would expect a lot of learners to turn it on and leave it on, at which point this becomes Option B with extra steps.

One thing to decide. Every phone interface on earth makes the hang-up button red. Our red is reserved for errors. I have drawn "Auflegen" neutral to keep that law intact; if you would rather it read as a call, say so and I will make the exception deliberate rather than accidental.

Build: small, plus a live audio level meter, which is a few lines of Web Audio.

6 · After the conversation: the debrief

The same in all three options, and the reason the whole feature is worth building. This is the Schreiben correction card, unchanged: coral marks on what you said, green on the fix, Himmelblau fix tiles. A correction looks identical whichever trainer produced it.

Rests inside one screen; the correction card expands under the app-wide expand rule.

Three things, in this order. Did you get the job done (the Leitpunkte, which is what the exam calls Aufgabenerfüllung); what you actually said, corrected; and which of the target Redemittel you reached for. Nothing else. There is no score out of a hundred on this screen, because a result is shown in one place per page and that place is the Verlauf.

The third goal is the interesting one. The AI can say the learner proposed a colleague but never asked for agreement. That is a genuinely useful thing to be told and no amount of multiple-choice scripting can produce it.

This is the existing correction module, not a fourth copy of it. src/features/writing/correction.tsx renders this today on three surfaces. Sprechen becomes the fourth caller, which is the whole reason the correction language must not drift.

And it feeds everything downstream. A spoken conversation with a correction and a Redemittel tally is a Verlauf row, a Fortschritt data point and a source of SRS-worthy weaknesses, exactly like a Kurz text. Today's Sprechen produces none of that.
